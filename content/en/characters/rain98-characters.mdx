## The Two Anchors of the Cast

Every visual novel needs at least one character who asks questions and one who refuses to answer them. Rain98 appears built on exactly that tension.

### The Player-Facing Protagonist

The showcase description refers to "their choices" changing "their world," which points to a protagonist who functions as the player's lens. In practice, that usually means a character who can observe, ask, and decide — but who cannot fix the weather. If Rain98 follows the standard visual novel contract, your protagonist will be defined less by backstory and more by the pattern of decisions you make for them.

### The Girl Who Wants Everything to End

This is the emotional center of the entire premise. A character who wants the world to end is not a simple antagonist; in a story like this, she is usually the character with the most information and the least hope. Note how the pitch is phrased — a girl who wants everything to end, in a city where the rain will not stop. Those two facts are almost certainly linked, and the story's job is to show you how.

### Tokyo as the Third Character

Late-90s Tokyo under permanent rain is not just scenery. In visual novels of this type, the environment behaves like a cast member: it sets the pace, limits where characters can go, and forces intimacy through shelter. When every conversation happens under an awning or on a stopped train, the setting is doing character work.

| Cast Element | Narrative Job | What to Watch For |
|---|---|---|
| The protagonist | Player lens and decision-maker | How much interior monologue you get |
| The girl at the center | Emotional engine and mystery | What she refuses to explain |
| Supporting figures | Pressure, contrast, and information | Who delivers bad news first |
| The city itself | Mood, pacing, and constraint | Where characters choose to stand |

## How the Cast Carries the Endless Rain Theme

A good visual novel cast is a set of arguments about the same idea. For Rain98, that idea is endurance — what you do when nothing gets better on schedule. Each character type tends to represent a different answer.

| Thematic Question | Character Type That Answers It | Typical Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Should we keep waiting? | The hopeful or stubborn figure | Makes plans anyway |
| Is this punishment? | The fatalistic figure | Rejects comfort |
| Who is to blame? | The investigator or skeptic | Digs into the past |
| Does it matter if no one remembers? | The nostalgic or archival figure | Hoards objects and stories |
| Can small kindnesses count? | The caretaker figure | Offers food, shelter, silence |

When you meet a new face in Rain98, ask which row of that table they belong to. It will tell you more about their role in the story than any bio will.

## A Practical Checklist for Reading the Cast as You Play

If you want to actually understand the Rain98 characters rather than just watch them, treat your first playthrough like fieldwork. The steps below work for almost any choice-driven visual novel.

| Step | What to Do | Why It Works |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Note who controls information | Characters who withhold are usually load-bearing |
| 2 | Track who initiates conversations | Initiative reveals desire |
| 3 | Log every promise made | Broken promises are the plot's engine |
| 4 | Watch reactions to the rain | Weather responses expose true moods |
| 5 | Compare two routes on one scene | Differences reveal author intent |
| 6 | Re-read the opening after finishing | Foreshadowing is usually hiding there |

Two habits are especially useful. First, keep a small notebook or notes app with one line per scene: who spoke, who stayed silent, what changed. Second, resist the urge to reload immediately after a bad outcome. In stories about an endless storm, consequences are the point, and a reloaded save can quietly erase the character development you were trying to study.

## Choices, Routes, and What They Do to the Cast

The showcase pitch explicitly frames Rain98 as a story where choices change the characters' world. That phrasing is worth unpacking, because choice-driven design changes how you should analyze a cast.

| Decision Type | Likely Effect on Characters | What It Reveals |
|---|---|---|
| Dialogue tone (kind vs. blunt) | Shifts trust and openness | How fragile the relationship is |
| Staying vs. leaving a scene | Alters who you learn about | Which character the writer protects |
| Offering help | Builds obligation and dependency | Whether care is transactional |
| Staying silent | Often the most powerful option | Who fills the silence |
| Committing to a plan | Locks in route direction | What the protagonist actually wants |

A useful test: pick the choice that feels least like you and play it out. Characters written with real depth respond differently to a protagonist who is unkind, and the contrast tells you whether the cast has range or just one scripted reaction.
