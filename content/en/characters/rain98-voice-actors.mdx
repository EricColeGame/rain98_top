## Why the Rain98 Cast Is Small — and Why That Matters

Rain98 has one of the tightest credited casts of any recent psychological suspense adventure, and that is precisely why the **Rain98 voice actors** carry so much weight. With a single heroine anchoring most of the dialogue, the game rises or falls on performance quality — and the team picked two of Japan's most distinctive talents to handle it. Below you'll find every confirmed **Rain98 voice actor**, the character they play, and what their work signals about the story's tone.

The game is a lo-fi psychological suspense adventure set in Tokyo in 1998, built around your day-to-day relationship with heroine Reina Amehara as the two of you inch toward ending the world together. That premise only works if Reina feels like a real person — funny, restrained one moment and volatile the next. A two-person cast means no ensemble to hide behind, so the vocal direction has to be precise.

That's the context you need before looking at the credits. This isn't a sprawling JRPG roster with dozens of names. It's a deliberately intimate production where every line of dialogue is doing narrative work, and where a single recast would change the entire feel of the game.

## Rio Tsuchiya as Reina Amehara

Rio Tsuchiya voices Reina Amehara, the heroine whose plan to end the world gives the game its title and its tension. If you follow Japanese game dubbing, you may recognize her as the voice behind Madoka Higuchi in *The Idolmaster Shiny Colors* — a role that shares Reina's blend of surface lightness and buried insecurity.

What makes her Reina work is range within restraint. Character profiles of the performance break it into four moving parts, and each one is doing something different for the story:

| Performance Layer | How It Shows Up | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Humor | Dry, offhand jokes that undercut the apocalypse talk | Keeps the tone lo-fi rather than melodramatic |
| Restraint | Long pauses, flat delivery in tense scenes | Makes the player lean in instead of being told how to feel |
| Volatility | Sudden sharp turns when Reina is pushed | Signals that the plan is not as controlled as she claims |
| Vulnerability | Quiet, small-voiced moments after conflict | Reminds you she's a person, not a plot device |

That combination is why Tsuchiya is arguably the most important of the Rain98 voice actors in practical terms. The demo reportedly includes over 300 illustrations, meaning the visual novel side of the game is dense — but illustrations don't create chemistry. Voice does. A three-hour demo is a long time to spend with one character, and Tsuchiya's job is to make those hours feel like a conversation rather than a monologue.

The demo also marks the first time players can hear Rain98's music with vocals, performed by Tsuchiya as Reina. That's a meaningful choice: it collapses the gap between character and soundtrack, so the songs feel like they belong to her rather than to the game around her.

## Mamiko Noto as Kyoko Yose

Mamiko Noto joins the cast as Kyoko Yose, appearing in both the trailer and the demo. Noto is a veteran of anime, games, and narration, born in Ishikawa Prefecture, with a career that spans acting, voice work, and singing. Her presence changes the temperature of the project.

Where Tsuchiya's Reina is quick and deflective, Noto's signature strength is control — a measured, almost soothing delivery that can turn unsettling depending on what the words actually say. Casting that voice as Kyoko gives the game a second axis. Reina pulls you forward into the plan; Kyoko is the character who makes you wonder what the plan really is.

For players who track casting choices, this is also a signal about the game's ambitions. Studios rarely spend a veteran name on a throwaway role in a two-person cast. The addition of Noto to the Rain98 voice actors roster, announced alongside the three-hour demo, suggests Kyoko has real narrative weight — not a cameo.

Community discussion around the demo has focused heavily on this pairing, with players noting that the trailer leans on the contrast between the two women rather than on scares. That's a deliberate marketing choice for a psychological suspense title: sell the relationship, not the horror.
