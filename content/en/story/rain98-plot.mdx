## The Rain98 Plot at a Glance: A Rain That Never Ends

Most visual novels try to hook you with a mystery. Rain98 hooks you with a mood: Tokyo, 1998, a rain that never stops, and a girl who wants everything to end. That one sentence is the spine of the Rain98 plot, and almost everything else in the game hangs off it — the wet streets you wander, the card games you play against a girl who barely seems to want to be there, and the choices that the store page insists will "change the world."

If you have been piecing the Rain98 plot together from trailers, store-page copy, and a three-hour demo, you are in good company. The game withholds far more than it shows. Here is what the material actually confirms, what it only hints at, and how the pieces appear to fit together.

That is a structural promise as much as a thematic one — it tells you the Rain98 plot is built to branch.

## Setting and Atmosphere: Why Tokyo 1998 Matters

The year is not decoration. Placing the Rain98 plot in 1998 Tokyo puts the story at the tail end of a decade defined by economic hangover, ambient anxiety about the approaching millennium, and a culture that was already deeply urban and deeply lonely. There are no smartphones in this world. Information travels slowly. People stand in the rain and wait.

Community reports from the demo consistently single out the atmosphere as the strongest element — the visual design, sound work, character art, and environmental detail. One player described the period representation as exceptionally well realized, and that tracks with what the store page shows: dense city streets, muted lighting, and a persistent downpour that turns every scene into a small act of endurance.

Rain in fiction is rarely just weather. Here it works as a clock. As long as it keeps falling, the story has not resolved. The moment it stops — if it stops — is the moment the Rain98 plot has to answer for itself.

## Characters: Reina Amehara and the Two Lonely Youths

Reina Amehara is the face of the game and, per the official material, its protagonist. She is voiced by Rio Tsuchiya, who also performs the vocal version of the featured song — a detail that matters more than it sounds, because it binds the character's voice to the game's emotional register rather than treating the music as separate decoration.

The second of the "two lonely youths" is the piece the marketing keeps deliberately vague. What the demo does make clear is that Reina is someone you spend time with rather than simply watch. Player experience from the demo describes playing card games against her, talking with her across multiple scenes, and gradually learning how much she is holding back.

There is also a telling bit of community behavior: the "I can fix her" jokes that surface in the Steam discussions. That reaction is a useful signal about how the Rain98 plot is being read. Audiences are meeting Reina as a character who seems to need saving, which is exactly the assumption a story like this tends to test.

### A note on speculation

It is worth separating confirmed material from fan inference. Reina's role, her voice actor, and her centrality are documented. Specific plot beats involving her, her motivations, or her fate are not. Treat any detailed theory about how the Rain98 plot resolves as speculation until the full game ships.

### Minigames and Side Content That Carry the Story

The demo expands the visual novel sections, adds more city areas to explore, and folds in a variety of interactions and minigames. The most discussed is Daifugo, a Japanese card game you play against Reina. On paper it is a diversion. In practice, it functions as characterization — a low-stakes activity that lets the story observe two people being ordinary together before anything dramatic happens.

Community reports also flag a snag: at least one player won a dozen or so Daifugo matches without completing the "win multiple Daifugo games" objective or receiving the associated sticker, and asked whether it was a bug or a hidden requirement. If you hit the same wall, it is likely a tracking issue rather than a skill issue.

The broader structure is worth noting for anyone mapping the Rain98 plot: the story is delivered through three channels at once — scripted dialogue, free exploration, and interactive side content. Miss the exploration and you miss texture. Skip the minigames and you miss the quiet character work that makes the dramatic beats land.

## FAQ About the Rain98 Plot

**What is the Rain98 plot about?**
The Rain98 plot follows two lonely youths in Tokyo in 1998, during a rain that never ends, one of whom — Reina Amehara — wants everything to end. Their choices are framed as world-changing.

**Is Rain98 a visual novel?**
It is a visual novel at its core, but it expands into exploration of the city and interactive minigames such as Daifugo. The story is told through dialogue scenes, free-roam moments, and side content rather than cutscenes alone.

**How much of the story is in the demo?**
The free demo runs over three hours and covers the opening through the first half of Chapter 2, available in nine languages. A later update is planned to add the second half of Chapter 2.

**Do choices actually change the Rain98 plot?**
The official description promises that choices will change the world, and the demo is built around player decisions. The full scope of branching and how many endings exist has not been confirmed, so treat specific theories as speculation until release.
