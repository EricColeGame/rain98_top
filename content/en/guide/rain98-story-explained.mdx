## What Rain98 Actually Is, in Plain Terms

Rain98 is a story-first visual novel with a very specific atmosphere: cold blue light, CRT-era dread, and a night city that looks like it was photographed in 1998 and left in a drawer. The premise is deliberately provocative — a time-travel story in which the stated goal is to go back into the past and end the world.

That is the hook, and it is also why so much of the conversation around the game is really a conversation about tone. Rain98 is not selling you a power fantasy about fixing history. It is selling you a mood: the sense that the present is exhausted and the past was, too. Everything else in the story — the technology, the timelines, the relationships — hangs off that idea.

## The Heisei-Era Backdrop That Shapes Everything

The Heisei period in Japan followed the collapse of the bubble economy and slid into what became known as the lost decades — a long stretch of stagnation, precarious work, and widespread institutional disappointment. That history is not set dressing in Rain98. It is the engine.

The late-1990s and early-2000s produced a wave of Japanese media that metabolized that anxiety into strange, ominous art. *Serial Experiments Lain*, *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, and *All About Lily Chou-Chou* are the reference points most often cited alongside Rain98, and the family resemblance is not subtle: introspective protagonists, hostile or indifferent systems, and endings that refuse to resolve cleanly.

Understanding that lineage is the fastest route to understanding what the game is doing. Rain98 is not nostalgic for the Heisei era in a cute, retro-filter way. It is nostalgic in the more uncomfortable sense — recognizing that the dread people felt then never actually went away, it just changed clothes.

| Influence | Era | What It Lends to Rain98 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Serial Experiments Lain* | Late 1990s | Networked alienation, dissolving identity, cold blue palette |
| *Neon Genesis Evangelion* | Mid-1990s | Apathy as armor, apocalyptic stakes, psychological interiority |
| *All About Lily Chou-Chou* | Early 2000s | Adolescent disaffection, quiet dread, digital escapism |

## Time Travel With a Twist: Two Eras, One Shared Frustration

Most time-travel stories run on contrast. Someone from a broken present travels to a golden past, or a comfortable present visits a grim future. Rain98 flips that by flattening the contrast: the world is bad now, and the world was bad then.

That is the smartest structural idea in the game, and it is the thing worth watching for if you are trying to follow the Rain98 story explained on your own terms. Two characters from different points in history meet, and neither one can accept their material conditions. Neither one has the luxury of believing things used to be better. The drama comes from watching them compare notes on two versions of the same disappointment.

| Dimension | The Past (Heisei Era) | The Present Day |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic mood | Post-bubble stagnation, lost decades | Precarious work, cost-of-living anxiety |
| Cultural output | Ominous, introspective, experimental | Nostalgic revival of that same mood |
| Character response | Withdrawal and fatalism | Cynicism and exhaustion |
| Shared question | Why does nothing change? | Why did nothing change? |

Notice what this structure does to the "end the world" premise. If both eras are already corroding, then destroying the world is not really an act of destruction — it is an act of refusal. It is one character's way of saying that a system which produces this much quiet misery does not deserve to continue. Whether the game endorses that or interrogates it is the central question the full release has to answer.

## The Serial Experiments Lain DNA, Explained

You can feel the *Lain* influence before anyone says the name. The promotional art alone tells the story: a visibly exhausted woman, cigarette in hand, standing on a balcony above a glowing night city with the moon hanging overhead. It is an image built entirely out of late-night isolation and screen light.

But the influence goes past the color grading. *Serial Experiments Lain* was fundamentally about the collapse of the boundary between the physical world and the network — about a girl who discovers that the self is not as solid as it seemed. Rain98 appears to be reaching for something adjacent: the idea that history is not a line you can travel along so much as a loop you keep re-entering.

| Surface Element | Where It Comes From | How Rain98 Uses It |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blue-washed night city | *Lain*'s visual language | Establishes perpetual late-night headspace |
| A tired, smoking protagonist | 1990s anime iconography | Signals burnout before a line of dialogue lands |
| The moon as recurring image | Heisei-era sci-fi and anime | A cold witness that exists in every era |
| Ambiguous technology | Cyberpunk and cyber-adjacent fiction | Makes time travel feel like a system, not magic |

Worth saying plainly: *Lain* is a stated influence, not a template. Rain98's structure — two people from two eras, arguing across time — is its own thing. Treating the game as a *Lain* clone will make you miss what it is actually doing.

## What the Demo Reveals — and What It Deliberately Withholds

The demo runs about three hours end to end, which is unusually generous for a vertical slice. Even so, preview coverage notes that a single hour in, the central question is still unanswered: how will these two characters respond to each other's inability to accept their circumstances?

That is not a flaw. It is pacing. Rain98 is spending its demo building the mood and the relationship rather than resolving the premise, and if you go in expecting a twist-reveal you will bounce off it. Player experience so far suggests the demo is best treated as a tone sample — a chance to decide whether you want to live inside this particular blue-lit headspace for a full-length game.

## How to Follow the Story Without Getting Lost

Rain98 is the kind of visual novel that rewards attention to repetition. Images come back. Lines of dialogue echo across eras. If you are trying to piece the Rain98 story explained together as you play, these habits help.

- **Track recurring objects, not just dialogue.** The moon, the cigarette, the balcony, the screen — anything that appears in both time periods is doing thematic work.
- **Note who is narrating each scene.** In two-era stories, the point of view is often the twist.
- **Read the environment text.** Visual novels bury characterization in background description far more than in spoken lines.
- **Resist the urge to pick a side early.** The game's whole argument is that both eras are compromised.
- **Keep a short timeline.** Write down what each character believes about the other's era before it gets corrected.

| Reading Habit | What It Unlocks |
| --- | --- |
| Watching for repeated imagery | Connects the two timelines thematically |
| Tracking who speaks vs. who narrates | Reveals perspective shifts early |
| Logging era-specific vocabulary | Shows how each character frames the other |
| Slowing down on static scenes | Catches foreshadowing the pacing hides |

One more thing worth flagging for anyone searching for the Rain98 story explained: the game's title and its rain imagery are not incidental. Rain in Heisei-era Japanese media is almost always shorthand for atmosphere as argument — weather that refuses to clear, a mood that will not lift. Expect the rain to mean something.

## FAQ

**Is Rain98 a horror game?**
Not in the jump-scare sense. It is closer to psychological and atmospheric dread — the same register as *Serial Experiments Lain* and *Neon Genesis Evangelion*. If you are looking for the Rain98 story explained as a straightforward scare-fest, that is not what this is.

**Do I need to play the demo to understand the full game?**
The demo is a slice, not a prologue summary, so it should not be mandatory. That said, three hours of demo is a substantial tone sample, and it is the best way to decide whether the pacing suits you before committing to a full playthrough.

**How long does the Rain98 demo take to finish?**
Roughly three hours, according to preview coverage. Reviewers who played about an hour reported still being in the setup phase, which tells you the game is in no hurry.

**Is Rain98 connected to Serial Experiments Lain?**
No — it is an original work that cites *Lain* as an influence. The shared DNA is aesthetic and thematic: blue light, networked alienation, and a protagonist who looks like she has not slept since 1998.

**Will the full game answer the "end the world" question?**
That is the whole bet the story is making. The demo deliberately withholds the answer, which means the payoff has to land in the full release — and that is exactly why the Rain98 story explained conversation is going to get a lot louder once it does.
