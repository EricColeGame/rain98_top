## What Is Rain98? A Quick Overview

Rain98 is a self-described "Lo-Fi psychological suspense" game from developer and publisher C#4R4CT3R, set in a rain-soaked Tokyo that refuses to stop crying. You share an apartment with Reina Amehara, a girl who introduces herself as "Angolmois" and who is quietly rooting for the end of everything. Your job is to talk with her, run her errands, wander the city together, and help her carry out a ritual meant to finish off the world.

The full game is still listed as a 2026 release on Steam, but a free demo arrived on October 16, 2026, alongside a new trailer at The MIX Fall Game Showcase. ## Rain98 Review: Story, Tone, and the Girl Called Angolmois

The premise is deceptively simple. You are dropped into a version of 1998 Tokyo where the rain never lets up, and the person you spend the most time with is a lonely girl who has decided the world deserves a finale. Reina is not written as a monster or a mystery box — she is written as someone pleasant to be around, which is exactly what makes the premise unsettling.

The game leans on contrast rather than jump scares. Blurry blue light, lo-fi beats, and rain against the window create genuine coziness; then a line of dialogue lands, and the comfort curdles. Community reports from the demo describe the same whiplash: you relax, you get attached, and then you remember what the two of you are actually building toward.

Where a lot of psychological horror games push you forward with threat, Rain98 pulls you forward with intimacy. The choices you make are less "fight or flee" and more "how much of yourself do you give this person." That framing is why the multiple-endings tag matters here — this is a story about accumulation, not reflexes.

### The 1998 Tokyo Setting Does Heavy Lifting

The period detail is not just wallpaper. Walking through a retro Shibuya with Reina is the game's main reward loop, and the environment sells the nostalgia: old signage, analog clutter, and a city that feels analog in a way modern Tokyo doesn't. If you grew up on late-90s media — or just like the aesthetic — the setting alone will carry you through the slower stretches.

## Gameplay: Requests, Stickers, and the Angel Passport

The structure alternates between novel-style reading segments and interactive sequences. In the reading segments, you learn more about Reina and the secrets she keeps. In the interactive segments, she asks you for things, and doing them well earns you stickers for her Angel Passport — the running tally that advances the ritual to end the world.

That mechanic is the cleverest thing in the demo. Progress is not measured in levels or currency; it's measured in small, tender memories. The game literally asks you to make someone's last days nicer in order to make sure there are no days after. Whether that lands as poignant or bleak probably depends on how much you like Reina by hour two.

| System | How It Works | Why It Matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Novel segments | Dialogue-driven story scenes inside the apartment | Builds the relationship and reveals Reina's backstory |
| Requests | Reina asks you to do specific tasks or favors | Drives interaction and keeps you engaged between scenes |
| Angel Passport | Earn stickers for special things you do for her | Tracks ritual progress and gates story advancement |
| City exploration | Leave the apartment and roam rainy 1998 Shibuya | Provides atmosphere, discovery, and a change of pace |
| Choice tracking | Decisions feed into multiple endings | Encourages replays and careful reading |

### What the Demo Gets Right

- The pacing is deliberate but rarely boring; scenes are short enough to keep momentum.
- The sticker system gives small actions emotional weight instead of busywork.
- Exploration breaks up dialogue-heavy stretches so the loop doesn't feel like a pure visual novel.
- The tone never tips into gratuitous shock — the dread is atmospheric, not graphic.

## Presentation: Lo-Fi Comfort with a Psychological Edge

Visually, Rain98 aims for "refined but soft" — clean character art, expressive animation, and a color palette that leans blue and gray without feeling washed out. Reina's animations do a lot of the emotional work; small idle movements and expressions sell her as a person rather than a sprite.

Sound design is the standout. Rain is layered continuously under lo-fi music, and the result is the kind of audio you could fall asleep to — which is precisely the trap the game is setting. Voice work is Japanese-only for full audio, with text localization in 16 languages, including English, French, German, Spanish, Italian, Polish, Russian, Korean, Simplified and Traditional Chinese, Thai, Indonesian, and Turkish.

## FAQ

**Is Rain98 out yet?**
No. The full game is planned for a 2026 release and is not yet available for purchase. A free demo of roughly three hours is available now on Steam, which is what this Rain98 review is based on.

**How long is the Rain98 demo?**
The developer announced it as a three-hour demo when it launched on October 16, 2026, alongside a new trailer at The MIX Fall Game Showcase. Your actual time will vary depending on how much you explore Shibuya and how thoroughly you chase stickers.

**Does Rain98 have multiple endings?**
Yes — multiple endings and "choices matter" are both listed among the game's official tags. The demo hints at consequence tracking through the Angel Passport ritual, though the full branching structure won't be clear until release.

**Is Rain98 a horror game?**
It's best described as psychological suspense with horror elements. The dread comes from atmosphere, music, and Reina's secret rather than gore or jump scares, which makes it approachable for players who usually avoid the genre.

**Do I need to play anything before Rain98?**
No. It's a standalone, single-player narrative game with no announced predecessor or required reading.
